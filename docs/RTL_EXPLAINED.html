RTL Files Explained

What each of the 15 Verilog files in the chip does, how data moves through them, and what you should see on the screen when you simulate. Written as if you were sitting next to a tutor.

Every number, table and waveform below was produced by simulating these exact files (on the Icarus simulator, since I do not have Xcelium or ModelSim here). The waveform pictures are drawn from the real simulation data, not sketched by hand. The Verilog itself is not modified anywhere in this page.

0. Read this first

The chip design is 15 Verilog files. Together they form one machine that reads a picture, does a large pile of multiply-and-add arithmetic, and answers "this is a 7". Reading 1 900 lines of Verilog cold is hard, so this page goes from the smallest piece upward: first the little arithmetic cell, then the grid made of those cells, then the memories that feed the grid, then the controller that tells everyone what to do, then the wrappers that let the outside world talk to it.

Every file gets the same treatment, so you can skim:

  1. The job in one sentence, then an everyday analogy.
  2. A diagram of what is inside and which wires go in and out.
  3. A worked example with real numbers (traced from the simulation, not invented).
  4. What to look for when you open the waveform: the signals worth adding and the shape they should have.

You do not need to remember any of this. Read Part A to Part E once in order, then jump to the testbench chapters, which tell you exactly what to look at on screen. The last two sections are a one-page checklist and a cheat sheet you can keep open while you run the simulation.

1. The whole chip in one picture

Before the files, here is the machine they build. Follow the blue line from left to right: a picture comes out of the activation memory, goes through a small reuse buffer, enters the grid of multiply-add cells (together called the systolic array), and its sums are added to any earlier partial sums and then squeezed back down to 8-bit numbers by the post-processing unit. Those 8-bit numbers are written back into the activation memory, ready to be the input of the next layer.

Part A. The arithmetic (files 1 to 4)

A neural-network layer is, at heart, thousands of "multiply two numbers, add to a running total" steps. Part A is the hardware that does those steps: first one cell (file 1), then the delay lines that time the data correctly (file 2), the grid made of 16 cells (file 3), and the wrapper that makes the grid easy to use (file 4).

1pe.v: the multiply-add cell

Filertl/pe.v · 63 linesCopies16 (one per grid position)Contains3 registers: weight 8 bit, activation 8 bit, running sum 32 bitUsesno other module

The job. "PE" stands for processing element. It remembers one weight (a number that was learned during training), and on every clock it multiplies the incoming activation (a pixel, or a number from the previous layer) by that weight and adds the product to a running total that arrives from the cell above. Then it hands the total downward and passes the activation along to its right-hand neighbour.

Analogy

A cashier desk on a conveyor belt. The price of the item is taped to the desk (the weight, written once). Each item that goes past is a pixel. The cashier multiplies, adds the amount to the bill slip that came from the desk before, and sends the slip on to the next desk, while also pushing the item itself on to the neighbouring desk to the right.

The three rules, once per clock (when en = 1)

RuleIn VerilogIn words
1. Pass the pixel onact_out <= act_in;The right-hand neighbour sees this pixel one clock later.
2. Multiply-addpsum_out <= psum_in + weight_reg * act_in;Product of the stored weight and the pixel, added to the total from above. This is one MAC (multiply-accumulate).
3. Load a weightif (weight_load) weight_reg <= weight_in;Normally the weight is frozen. Only while weight_load is 1 does the register copy the value arriving from above. The value is also visible on weight_out, so the weights ripple downward one cell per clock.

Why the file looks the way it does

Why "signed"?
Weights and pixels can be negative. If the multiplier treated 8-bit values as 0…255, then −3 would be read as 253 and the answers would be wrong. The first version of this PE in your report did this and only passed because every test number was positive. The corrected cell is declared signed. If you break it on purpose, tb_pe prints [FAIL] act=7 psum_in=0 -> psum_out=65515 (exp -21) (see the testbench chapter).
Why a 32-bit sum for 8-bit inputs?
One 8×8 product needs 16 bits. Adding 64 of them (a full layer-1 neuron) can reach a little over a million, which needs about 22 bits. Using 32 gives safe head-room and matches what software calls int32.
Why weight_out is just a wire from the weight register
It re-uses the register that already holds the weight as one stage of a shift chain, so loading 16 weights costs no extra flip-flops.
Why en?
When the chip is idle the whole array is told en = 0. Nothing toggles, so (in a real chip) almost no power is burned. In this design en is wired to the controller's busy.
What to look for

Open tb_pe in the waveform viewer (see "Testbench 1"). Add act_in, psum_in, act_out, psum_out. You should see every output change exactly one clock after its input, and psum_out equal to psum_in + weight × act_in. Figure 26 shows it.

2skew_delay.v: the staggered start

Filertl/skew_delay.v · 52 linesCopies2 (one before the grid, one after)Containsshift registers: 48 flip-flops at the input, 192 at the outputUsesno other module

The job. Delay lane number i by a different number of clocks so that the data forms a diagonal wave. The same file is used twice: DESKEW=0 delays lane i by i clocks (before the grid) and DESKEW=1 delays lane i by 3−i clocks (after the grid, to undo the stagger).

Why the grid needs a diagonal. Look back at Figure 3: the running total takes one clock to fall from one row to the next. Row 1 can only add its product to row 0's total if row 1 receives the matching pixel exactly one clock after row 0 did. Row 2 two clocks after, and so on. But the outside world hands over all four pixels of one picture-slice in the same clock. Something has to hold back the later ones.

Analogy

The start of a relay race around a track where the four lanes begin at different points: all four runners are told "go" at the same moment, but the one in lane 3 has three extra steps to take before reaching the common line. A marching band turning a corner does the same: the outer row keeps walking while the inner row waits.

How the file is written

One generate for loop makes four lanes. Lane 0 has delay 0, so it becomes a plain wire (assign dout = din): no hardware. Lane i otherwise gets a shift register that is i words long. New data goes in at the least-significant end and the oldest word is read from the most-significant end. The register also has a synchronous reset and the shared en.

InstanceParameterLane delaysLane widthFlip-flops
u_in_skewDESKEW=00, 1, 2, 3 clocks8 bit(0+1+2+3)×8 = 48
u_out_deskewDESKEW=13, 2, 1, 0 clocks32 bit(3+2+1+0)×32 = 192

Why the result side needs the mirror image. Column 0 of the grid finishes first and column 3 finishes three clocks later (they are the end of a diagonal wave too). The next stage wants all four results of one picture in the same clock, so the early columns are delayed: column 0 by 3, column 1 by 2, column 2 by 1, column 3 by 0.

What to look for

In the chip simulation add tb_dnn_player.g_reg.dut.u_core.u_core.act_skewed (the output of the input skew). It is a 32-bit bus made of four bytes. During a pass each row's byte appears one clock after the row above it (row 1 one clock after row 0, row 2 two clocks after row 0, row 3 three clocks after row 0). In the real run many of the first pictures have 0 in their top pixels, so you will see the staircase on the non-zero bytes: for example the first non-zero byte is row 2 (10 in 00100000) and the row 3 byte (67) appears one clock later. Between passes the bus is all zeros. That staircase is the skew.

3systolic_array.v: the 4×4 grid

Filertl/systolic_array.v · 90 linesCopies1Contains16 PEs (rows 0..3 × columns 0..3) and nothing elseUsespe (file 1)

The job. Wire 16 PEs together so that pixels flow left-to-right, running totals flow top-to-bottom, and weights can be loaded top-to-bottom. It contains no control and no memory of its own. It is a pure mesh of wires and cells.

The word systolic comes from the heart: data is pumped through the grid rhythmically, one hop per clock, and each cell only ever talks to its immediate neighbours. That is the whole trick: no cell has to fetch anything from a big memory in the middle of the computation, so it is fast and uses little energy.

What one pass computes

Suppose the four weights in column 0 are W[0][0]…W[3][0] and a vector of four pixels a0…a3 flows in. After the wave has crossed the grid, the bottom of column 0 delivers W[0][0]·a0 + W[1][0]·a1 + W[2][0]·a2 + W[3][0]·a3. This is one four-term dot product. Four columns give four dot products per vector, each clock a new vector can enter.

When does each cell do real work?

For one vector that enters at time τ, PE(r, c) multiplies it at τ + r + c. A 4×4 grid therefore has a diagonal "front":

col 0col 1col 2col 3
row 0τ+0τ+1τ+2τ+3
row 1τ+1τ+2τ+3τ+4
row 2τ+2τ+3τ+4τ+5
row 3τ+3τ+4τ+5τ+6

The last cell, PE(3,3), is busy at τ+6, so its result is available at τ+7. That is where the "latency 7" of the next file comes from.

How the weights get inside

While weight_load = 1 all 16 weight registers shift one row downward each clock, and the top row takes the new numbers from wgt_in. One 32-bit word carries one row of the tile (4 columns × 8 bits). Four loading clocks fill the grid. Because the first word ends up at the bottom, the controller must send tile row 3 first and row 0 last:

after load clockword fed inrow 0 holdsrow 1 holdsrow 2 holdsrow 3 holds
1tile row 3row 3oldoldold
2tile row 2row 2row 3oldold
3tile row 1row 1row 2row 3old
4tile row 0row 0row 1row 2row 3

(You will see this in dnn_ctrl.v: the weight address counts down, w_ptr + ROWS − 1 − ld_cnt.)

The one strange-looking line of Verilog

You will see things like act_w[(r*(COLS+1)+c)*DATA_WIDTH +: DATA_WIDTH]. It looks scary, but it only means: "the numbered slice of a very long wire". The long wire act_w is all the pixel connections of the grid laid end to end. The slice number is r*(COLS+1)+c, and +: DATA_WIDTH means "start at that slice's first bit and take 8 bits". PE(r, c) reads slice (r, c) as its act_in and writes slice (r, c+1) as its act_out: the output of one is literally the input of its right neighbour. The same trick connects the sums and the weights downward. The extra slices at the edges (the right of column 3, the bottom of row 3 for weights) are simply not used.

What to look for

In the chip run, tb_dnn_player.g_reg.dut.u_core.u_core.u_array.g_row[0].g_col[0].u_pe is PE(0,0). Add its weight_reg: it stays constant for a whole pass and changes only during the four clocks of the load phase (state LDW). Add psum_out of PE(0,0) and of PE(3,0): the lower one lags the upper one by three clocks.

4sa_core.v: grid, with doors and a "valid" flag

Filertl/sa_core.v · 104 linesCopies1 (named u_core inside file 9)Containsinput skew, grid, output de-skew, a 7-stage valid shift registerUsesskew_delay (2) × 2, systolic_array (3)

The job. Present the grid as a simple device: "put one vector of four pixels in each clock; seven clocks later one vector of four 32-bit results comes out each clock, with a flag telling you when the results are real."

Why there is a valid flag at all

The grid has no idea whether a number going through it is real. Between passes the input is forced to zeros and zeros flow through just like real data. The flag tells the next stage which clocks to keep and which to ignore. The flag's journey is a plain row of 7 flip-flops (vld_pipe); it is as long as the data path so they stay in step.

A complete example, small enough to check by hand

To keep the numbers readable I simulated this very file with a 2×2 grid (the latency is then 2+2−1 = 3). The weights are W = [[5, 6], [7, 8]]; two vectors go in back-to-back: a0 = [1, 2] and a1 = [3, 4].

By hand: vector a0 gives column 0: 1×5 + 2×7 = 19, column 1: 1×6 + 2×8 = 22. Vector a1 gives column 0: 3×5 + 4×7 = 43, column 1: 3×6 + 4×8 = 50. Now the real simulation trace, one row per clock (c = 0 is the clock a0 is presented):

Read it like a detective:

  • c = 1: PE(0,0) has multiplied the first pixel of a0 (1) by its weight (5) = 5. Row 1 is still waiting; its pixel (2) arrives one clock late: see the "after skew" column.
  • c = 2: PE(1,0) = 19 = 5 (the total that fell from above) + 2 × 7. That is the finished column-0 result for a0, sitting at the bottom of column 0. Meanwhile PE(0,0) = 15 = 3 × 5 is already working on a1.
  • c = 3: PE(1,1) = 22: the column-1 result for a0 is ready one clock after the column-0 result. The de-skew delayed 19 by one clock so that both 19 and 22 appear together on res_vec while res_vld rises. This is the 3-clock latency.
  • c = 4: the second vector's results [43, 50] follow immediately, one per clock, with no gap. That is the throughput of 1 vector per clock.

The real 4×4 chip does exactly this with a delay of 7 instead of 3, and 4 numbers per vector instead of 2.

What to look for

On the real chip, add tb_dnn_player.g_reg.dut.u_core.core_res_vld (this is res_vld of sa_core). Compare with u_ctrl.feed_vld (the controller's "a vector is on its way" flag): core_res_vld is a solid block of 8 clocks (the 8 pictures of the batch, back to back). It starts 8 clocks after feed_vld rises (1 clock for the reuse buffer's output register plus the array's 7). If the block is shorter than 8 or has holes, something upstream is wrong. Figure 12 shows the real block.

Part B. Memory (files 5 and 6)

A multiply-add grid is useless if it cannot be fed. These two files hold the numbers: a generic memory block that is used five times (file 5), and a small cache that stops the biggest memory from being read over and over (file 6).

5sram_sdp.v: a wall of numbered pigeonholes

Filertl/sram_sdp.v · 45 linesCopies5 (four in file 9, one inside file 6)Containsan array of words, one write port, one read portUsesno other module

The job. Store words. You can write one word and read one word in the same clock, at different addresses ("simple dual port": one port only writes, the other only reads). It is a synchronous memory: the data you asked for in clock n shows up on rd_data in clock n+1.

Analogy

A wall of numbered pigeonholes with one clerk. You hand over a slip with a number on it, and the clerk comes back with the note from that hole on the next clock tick. You cannot have the note in the same tick.

How a word is packed (a real one)

The very first word the testbench writes into the weight memory is fbfd0400 (hexadecimal). Split it into bytes from the right (the least significant byte is lane 0):

Two details worth knowing

  • No reset on the data. Only the output register rd_data is reset. The stored words are not, because real SRAMs have no reset. That is why the host must write the weights before use.
  • Read and write at the same address in the same clock returns the old value. The accumulator relies on never doing that to the same word, which the controller guarantees.
What to look for

Pick any memory, e.g. tb_dnn_player.g_reg.dut.u_core.u_wgt_mem, and add rd_en, rd_addr, rd_data. Every time rd_en is high, check that rd_data shows the right word one clock later. If you ever see data on the same clock as the address, you are looking at the wrong signal.

6lrb.v: the "Local Reuse Buffer"

Filertl/lrb.v · 96 linesCopies1Containsone small SRAM (8 × 32 bit), a source selector and an output registerUsessram_sdp (file 5)

The job. The same input vectors are needed again and again: once for every group of 4 output neurons. Without a buffer the big activation memory would be read 4 times (layer 0) or 3 times (layer 1) for the same data. Reading a big memory costs far more energy than reading a tiny one, so the first time a vector is read, a copy is kept in this small buffer, and the following passes read the copy.

Analogy

You are cooking four dishes that all need the same recipe card. The first time you fetch the card from the library across the road, then you pin a copy on the kitchen wall. For the other three dishes you glance at the wall.

Real waveform: fill, then replay

What it saves, in numbers

For one batch of 8 pictures through the whole network the simulation measured: 160 reads of the big activation SRAM and 448 reads of the small buffer. Without the buffer all 160 + 448 = 608 reads would have gone to the big memory. So 448 of 608 reads (74 %) are redirected to the cheap memory. You can reproduce those numbers from the register map: PERF_ACT_RD reads 0x000000a0 (160) and PERF_LRB_RD reads 0x000001c0 (448). Where they come from: layer 0 has 16 K-slices × 8 pictures = 128 first-time reads and 16 × 3 × 8 = 384 replays; layer 1 has 4 × 8 = 32 first reads and 4 × 2 × 8 = 64 replays; 128 + 32 = 160 and 384 + 64 = 448.

What to look for

Add u_ctrl.act_rd_en, u_ctrl.lrb_wr_en, u_ctrl.lrb_rd_en, u_ctrl.feed_sel_lrb. In the first pass of every K-slice act_rd_en is an 8-clock block; in the next passes of the same slice it is lrb_rd_en. Never both in the same clock. Over the whole batch (zoom out) you will see act_rd_en blocks rare (once per K-slice) and lrb_rd_en blocks three times as frequent, exactly like the lrb_rd_en row of Figure 13.

Part C. The brain (file 7)

7dnn_ctrl.v: the foreman with a clipboard

Filertl/dnn_ctrl.v · 379 lines (the biggest)Copies1 (u_ctrl inside file 9)Containslayer descriptor table (4 layers × 16 fields × 16 bit = 1 024 bits), loop counters, address generators, 5-state machineUsesno other module

The job. The host does not tell the chip "load this tile now, run it now". It writes, once, a short work order for every layer (the descriptor) and presses START. This file reads each work order and runs all the loops by itself: for every layer, for every tile, load the weights, stream the pictures, wait for the result, move on. At the very end it raises done.

Analogy

A foreman with a clipboard. Each page of the clipboard is one layer's work order ("batch of 8, 16 slices, 4 column groups, weights start here, results go there, scale by this much"). He walks through every task on the page in a fixed order, shouts the right shelf numbers to the clerks (memories), and ticks each step. When the last page is done he rings a bell (done).

2. The loops (the "schedule")

One layer of the network is a big matrix multiplication. The weight matrix is cut into 4×4 tiles. The controller visits every tile exactly once, in this order:

for layer in 0 .. n_layers-1:
  for kt in 0 .. KT-1:           # outer: which 4-wide slice of the input
    for nt in 0 .. NT-1:         # inner: which group of 4 outputs
      PASS(kt, nt):
        LDW   - load the 4x4 weight tile (4 clocks)
        COMP  - stream the M pictures' slice kt through the array (M clocks)
        DRAIN - wait until all M results have been stored
        if kt == KT-1: results go through the PPU into the activation SRAM
        else:          results go into the accumulator SRAM (added to earlier partial sums)

Putting nt as the inner loop is the clever choice: the pictures' slice kt stays the same for NT passes in a row, so the reuse buffer (file 6) can serve passes 2…NT from its copy.

Part D. Finishing a layer (files 8, 9, 10)

The grid produces big 32-bit sums, one tile at a time. Before the next layer can use them, three things must happen: sums from the different K-slices must be added together, the bias must be added, and the big number must be shrunk back to an 8-bit number. File 8 does the shrinking, file 9 organises everything (and is where the pieces of Part A to C are plugged together), file 10 counts what happened.

8dnn_ppu.v: bias, scale, clamp

Filertl/dnn_ppu.v · 108 linesCopies1Contains4 parallel lanes, each with 3 pipeline registers; a 3-stage valid flagUsesno other module

The job. "PPU" = post-processing unit. It turns each finished 32-bit sum into the 8-bit number the next layer will use:

y = clamp( ( (sum + bias) × mult + round ) >> shift )

Analogy

Turning a raw exam score into a school mark between 0 and 127. First add the bonus marks (bias). Then convert the scale, for example "multiply by 0.0013" (mult and shift: multiply by a whole number, then divide by a power of two, which is just dropping bits; this avoids a hardware divider and floating point). Round to the nearest mark, and finally never go above 127; and if the rule says so (ReLU), never go below 0.

The job. This is the integration file. It instantiates the controller, the four memories, the reuse buffer, the arithmetic core, the PPU and the counters, connects them, and adds the three pieces of logic that belong to none of them:

  1. K accumulation. A layer with 64 inputs is computed 4 inputs at a time (16 K-slices). The results of the 16 slices must be added up. The array gives one slice's contribution; this file adds it to the running total that is stored in the accumulator memory.

Writing wide words: the "beat" trick

The bus is 32 bits wide but the bias memory's words are 128 bits (four 32-bit numbers). So a wide word is written in several 32-bit pieces called beats, least significant first. The earlier beats are parked in a staging register; the write of the last beat commits the whole word to the memory. For the weight and activation windows a word is 32 bits so one write commits at once. The real first bias word of layer 0 is written like this (lines 265–268 of prog.hex):

Reading a result back

The activation memory has a read port that is shared with the engine, so a host read is a three-step handshake, which you will see over and over in prog.hex (and in the testbench's opcodes 01, 05, 03):

  1. Write the word number to ACT_RD_ADDR (0x0003). The register block pulses act_re for one clock.
  2. Poll STATUS until bit 3 (rd_valid) is 1. (It is 0 while the read is in flight, one clock.)
  3. Read 0x0010. You get 32 bits = four signed 8-bit scores.

Real example, the first one after the first batch: the word is 0xf6e8f2f8, i.e. bytes f8, f2, e8, f6 = −8, −14, −24, −10. Those are the scores of digits 0, 1, 2, 3 for picture 0. The next two words give digits 4–7 and 8–9 (plus two unused lanes). The largest score of picture 0 is 34, digit 7: the chip says "7".

12rst_sync.v: the reset cleaner

The job. The external reset button can be pressed or released at any instant, not in step with the clock. If a thousand flip-flops saw the release at slightly different moments, some would start working one clock before others and the chip could wake up in an inconsistent state. This file takes the raw reset and produces a version whose release is aligned to a clock edge.

Analogy

A turnstile that only turns on the beat of a metronome. People (the reset button) can crowd at the gate whenever they want, but they are let through only on the beat, all together.

The real behaviour

  • This is the only asynchronous reset in the whole design (the file header calls it the one documented exception). Every other register uses a synchronous reset: if (!rst_n) inside always @(posedge clk).
  • The clock has to be running while reset is held, otherwise the synchronous resets inside the design never get an edge to act on.
What to look for

Add rst_n_i (the testbench's reset), g_reg.dut.rst_n (the cleaned one) and g_reg.dut.g_rs.u_rst_sync.sr. The cleaned reset must rise on a clock edge and two edges after the raw one. If you see them rise together you probably added the same net twice.

14axil_reg_bridge.v: the AXI4-Lite interpreter

Filertl/axil_reg_bridge.v · 127 linesCopies1, only in the AXI buildContainsa 4-state machine and a few registersUsesno other module

The job. A real processor (ARM, RISC-V…) does not use the simple "write strobe + address + data" bus of file 11. It talks AXI4-Lite, an industry-standard protocol with five independent channels and a "valid / ready" handshake on each. This file is the interpreter that turns an AXI4-Lite transaction into one clean strobe for the register block, then answers the processor the way the protocol demands.

ChannelDirectionCarries
AW (write address)master → chipawaddr + awvalid / awready
W (write data)master → chipwdata, wstrb + wvalid / wready
B (write response)chip → masterbresp + bvalid / bready: "your write is done"

The handshake rule

A piece of information moves only on a clock edge where VALID and READY are both 1. The sender raises VALID when it has something; the receiver raises READY when it can take it; neither may withdraw until the transfer happens. It works like handing a parcel across a counter: the parcel only changes hands when one person holds it out and the other has their hands ready.

Simplifications to know about

  • Byte strobes (wstrb) are ignored: every write stores a full 32-bit word. The driver only does 32-bit stores.
  • Responses are always OKAY (2'b00), even for an unused address.
  • The address is awaddr[17:2]: AXI uses byte addresses and the registers use word addresses, so byte address = 4 × word address. The bottom two bits are ignored.
What to look for

Only in an AXI run (parameter AXIL = 1). Add the five channels' VALID and READY signals. For every write you should see awvalid, wvalid and then, within a clock or two, bvalid; for every read arvalid followed by rvalid. Nothing should ever wait forever: the testbench has a 1 000-clock safety limit and prints [FAIL] AXI write: no B response if the bridge ever stops answering.

The waveform

Here is the same run drawn from the simulator's recorded signals, first 130 ns (13 clocks). The orange boxes in the bottom row show what the testbench is doing; the green boxes show what each multiply-add should yield.

What to look for
  1. clk is a regular square wave, 10 ns period. rst_n is low for the first 16 ns and then stays high (it only drops again much later, at the reset test). en is high the whole time.
  2. weight_load is a short one-clock pulse before each new weight (at 16 ns, 56 ns, 86 ns and 126 ns here). It is 0 otherwise, even when weight_in is changing.
  3. weight_out (the PE's stored weight) is X (unknown) until the first clock edge, then 0, then it changes to the new weight one clock after each weight_load pulse: 5, then −3, then −128. It does not change when weight_in changes without weight_load.
  4. act_out is a copy of act_in delayed by exactly one clock. Put a ruler across: the shape repeats one grid line later.
  5. psum_out follows the green boxes: 15, 30, 55, then 0 (a gap clock), −21, 26 …, one clock after the corresponding inputs. Each value equals psum_in + weight × act_in of the previous clock.
  6. Every output is registered, so nothing ever changes between clock edges. If you see a change in the middle of a clock period, you are looking at the testbench's own signals (they change 1 ns after the edge), not the PE's outputs.

Testbench 2: tb_dnn_player.v (the whole chip)

What it does in one sentence: acts as a tiny CPU: reads a list of register operations from build/prog.hex, performs them on the chip one at a time (load weights, load pictures, press START, wait for DONE, read the scores), and checks every value read against the answer Python worked out in advance.

Where "expected" comes from

The expected values in the READ_EXPECT lines are not guesses: they were computed by sw/golden.py, a Python model that does the same integer arithmetic as the chip (file 8's formula, 8-bit weights, the same rounding). The Python script compile.py wrote them into prog.hex when it made the file. So the testbench does not know what a "7" is: it only knows "for this picture the chip must return exactly these 12 numbers". That is why PASSED means the chip equals the Python model bit for bit, for all 360 pictures.

The accuracy figure (96.94 % correct digits) comes later, from report.py, which reads the read-back file and compares the guessed digit with the true label. The testbench itself never prints an accuracy.

The run, step by step

  1. Load the program.

    The file is read into the prog[] array. If the file is missing or empty the testbench stops at once with PLAYER: program file empty or not found followed by *** PLAYER FAILED ***, so a wrong file name can never look like a pass.

  2. Reset.

    The reset input is held low for 4 clocks, released, and the testbench waits 4 more clocks (the reset cleaner of file 12 needs 2).

  3. Check that it is the right chip.

    Lines 1–8 read the ID and the 7 configuration registers and compare them with the expected constants (file 11's table).

  4. Fill the chip.

    Lines 9–361: weights, biases and descriptors for the two layers, and N_LAYERS = 2. These are 353 ordinary writes.

  5. Batch 0: pictures in, START, wait.

    128 writes put 8 pictures into the activation memory, one write to CTRL starts the engine, and WAIT_DONE polls STATUS about 1 773 times while the engine runs its 1 771 clocks.

  6. Read the answers.

    For each of the 8 pictures, 3 words (digits 0–3, 4–7, 8–9). Each word is read with the three-op handshake of file 11 and compared.

  7. Repeat for batches 1 to 44.

    The weights stay in the chip: only the 128 picture writes, START, the wait and the 24 comparisons repeat.

  8. Summary.

    The testbench prints the totals and the verdict.

What PASSED proves, and what it does not

It proves
  • The design, as written in Verilog, computes the same 1 080 result words (360 pictures × 3) as the independent Python model.
  • Every register, memory window and status flag the program touches behaves as documented.
  • The same holds when the chip is accessed through the AXI4-Lite bridge (AXIL=1), with and without random delays.
It does not prove
  • Anything about speed in a real chip: the simulation uses ideal zero-delay logic. Genus and Innovus tell you timing.
  • That the synthesised gates behave identically: that needs a gate-level simulation, which this project does not include.
  • That the pictures outside the 360-picture test set are classified correctly (that is the accuracy question, answered by report.py).
